Friday Night Drummers / Music, dance & storytelling

Through music, dance, and storytelling, Friday Night Drummers shared rhythms from West African nations including Guinea, Mali, and Sierra Leone. They explored the cultural significance of music and dance and their importance to community and everyday life.

The evening concluded with an interactive dance session: students and staff joined the performers on stage to learn a dance routine. A shared moment of rhythm, learning, and participation.

A memory in print / TWASblog

Voices of the Diaspora: An Intergenerational Conversation

Event review by Lise Watson · Published March 29, 2024

Gardiner Museum, Toronto
111 Queen’s Park, Toronto, Ontario, Canada M5S 2C7 · March 21, 2024 · 5:30–8:00 pm

The evening brought artists and audiences together around Magdalene Odundo: A Dialogue with Objects, an exhibition of the Kenyan-born British artist’s work. Watson’s review explores the connections between visual art, African and diasporic traditions, music, and movement.

Gardiner Museum venue information ↗

A conversation across generations

Collette “Coco” Murray moderated a panel with Enas Satir, Kobèna Aquaa-Harrison, Kwanza Msingwana, and Tura Cousin Wilson. The artists discussed Odundo’s influence on their creative practices.

From the exhibition to Calabash

Following the conversation, Coco Collective performed and Murray led the audience through the exhibition in a moving procession. The evening also introduced Calabash, with an opening oration by Kwanza Msingwana, African instruments, and Murray’s dance bringing Odundo’s influence into performance.

The review names me, Christian Powlette, among Coco Collective’s performers, alongside Kwanza Msingwana, Kobèna Aquaa-Harrison, Sherwin Charles, Kimberely Charles, and Nicole Powlette.

Watson celebrates the event’s welcoming approach to cultural participation, showing how conversation, drumming, and dance can invite audiences into a shared museum experience.

The Robert McLaughlin Gallery / Oshawa

The Robert McLaughlin Gallery

Stories, rhythm, and a little wonder.

RMG Friday: From the Ground Up

A little piece of the program for RMG Friday on August 8, 2025, from 7 to 10 p.m. at The Robert McLaughlin Gallery, 72 Queen Street, Civic Centre, Oshawa, Ontario, Canada L1H 3Z3. The evening brought storytelling, folk tales, dance, and live percussion into the gallery.

Miss Coco Murray’s Coco Moko Jumbie performance was accompanied by West African drums and percussion. The gallery introduced the stilt-dance tradition through its connections to masquerade, emancipation, and contemporary Caribbean culture. Durham Storytellers’ Dianne Chandler, Kesha Christie, Enid De Coe, and Brenda Beck also shared tales drawing on traditions from the Caribbean, Ireland, India, and beyond.

Coco Collective / Calabash

Dance, percussion & storytelling.

Calabash, created by Collette “Coco” Murray, uses dance and live percussion to explore nourishment, everyday relationships, and connections across the African and Caribbean diaspora.

Calabash debuted at the Gardiner Museum on March 21, 2024 as a drum-and-dance work inspired by Magdalene Odundo. Explore the event review and photographs ↓

I’m credited among the musical composers for Calabash on Miss Coco Murray’s performance page, and among its performers in Dance Ontario’s DanceWeekend ’25 program.

Dance Ontario / February 22, 2025

City Dance Corps

Calabash at DanceWeekend ’25

Coco Collective performed as part of the DanceWeekend ’25 Dance Party with DJ Grouch, presented in partnership with dance Immersion.

Venue: City Dance Corps · 950 Dupont Street, Toronto, Ontario, Canada.

The program describes Calabash as a story told through dance and live percussion, exploring how vessels connect everyday social life and local food sovereignty in African-diasporic communities. The calabash links African and Caribbean traditions across the Atlantic: gathering, holding, carrying, and offering.

Collette “Coco” Murray

About Coco Collective

The 2025 program introduces Coco Collective as an intergenerational, multidisciplinary ensemble with a decade of work in African and Caribbean arts. Under Murray’s artistic direction, it brings together live African-indigenous percussion, West African and Caribbean folk dance, audience education, and community projects.

Presented with dance Immersion

Founded by Vivine Scarlett in 1994, dance Immersion is a charitable nonprofit supporting African-diasporic dancers and dance through performance opportunities, artistic development, and connections among artists in Canada and internationally.

Emancipation Day / Freedom Train

TTC / Toronto Transit Commission

Celebration & remembrance.

The TTC’s Underground Freedom Train Ride honours the journey of formerly enslaved people to Canada and the significance of the Underground Railroad.

Presented in partnership with Blackhurst Cultural Centre, the gathering brings people together through singing, drumming, and reflection on Emancipation Day. The Toronto Star video above documents the 12th annual ride, which departed Vaughan Metropolitan Centre Station late on July 31, 2025 and reached Union Station in Toronto in the early hours of August 1.

The djembe / Wula Drum

Rhythm as a shared language.

In its mission statement, Wula Drum describes the djembe as an instrument that brings people together to communicate, celebrate, and motivate. Its work connects West African instruments, performance, and teaching with music and dance education, environmental awareness, and sustainable livelihoods for artists and drum makers in Guinea and the United States.

Wula traces the djembe’s wider international recognition to touring Guinean ensembles such as Les Ballets Africains. Founded in 2006, the company works with salaried artists in Guinea to develop its instruments. Its name was chosen by artistic director M’Bemba Bangoura: Wula means “deep inside the forest” in the Susu language.
